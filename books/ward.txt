WARD
A Novella
Being the fifth night of Bikram Dey
FRACTURED CYNICISM
Ward (n.):
1.               A division of a hospital or institution.
2.               To ward off: to deflect, to keep at a distance.
3.               A person under the care or protection of another.
4.               A division. A separation. The act of being partitioned.
---
Three books ago, a man named Bikram Dey made three wrong calls at a football match.
He survived.
Two books ago, he made one correct call at a combat tournament.
He survived.
One book ago, he protected an election.
The book before that, he investigated an institutional conspiracy.
Between them, his body filed comprehensive dissenting opinions.
He has been negotiating with that filing ever since.
This story is about what surviving cost.
It takes place over thirty-nine hours.
He checks in on a Tuesday.
By Thursday morning either he leaves or he doesn't.
The word for the condition between those two facts is the title.

CHAPTER ONE
The Intake
Maitri Wellness and Rehabilitation Centre, Barrackpore — Tuesday, 3:00 PM
He had agreed to come voluntarily. This was the fact he kept in front of everything else like a man carrying something fragile through a crowd — held out, kept clear, not allowed to touch anything. The something fragile was not the decision. The something fragile was his left side. The ribs that had been separated by impact weeks ago. The shoulder that had begun to disagree with the weight of his own arm. The specific threshold below which the body stopped negotiating and started filing formal complaints.
He had agreed. He had made the appointment himself. He had taken the auto from Ultadanga to Sealdah and the train from Sealdah to Barrackpore and the facility's pickup vehicle from the station. The AIFSFO board's recommendation had been that he needed rest and recovery. The alternative was institutional scrutiny of his judgment. The appearance of voluntary cooperation with medical recommendation was the path that protected both his certification and his autonomy.
He told himself this at regular intervals on the way and understood, in the part of him that had been trained to identify the difference between orientation and fabrication, that the regularity of the telling was itself information.
The facility's intake process took forty-seven minutes. Forms, photographs, a brief assessment with the duty nurse, the surrender of his phone to the storage locker — standard procedure, voluntary patients could access personal items during scheduled periods, the nurse explained this with the specific warmth of someone who has explained it many times and has not yet stopped meaning it. His wallet. His keys. His officiating credentials, which he kept in his jacket pocket always and which the nurse looked at briefly and did not comment on.
The fourth optometrist's report. He kept that too. The nurse did not ask about it. The imaging from the election night incident, which had shown the rib damage in clinical detail. He had not brought those. The AIFSFO medical panel had those. The records he was keeping — the ones that documented his functional degradation below forty percent — those were the ones the facility might ask for next.
He was shown to Room 7, Ward A, first floor. Standard single room. A bed, a chair, a window overlooking the garden, a bathroom with a door that locked from the inside which the nurse confirmed twice. The confirmation itself was institutional information — it told him something about what previous patients had needed to be reassured about.
He understood the implications.
The garden was exactly what the brochure had said. He had read the brochure three times in the weeks before admission, not because he doubted it but because reading it was something to do while he was not making the appointment. The garden: maintained, planted with things that were chosen for their softness, their non-sharpness, their capacity to receive a person sitting on a bench at any hour without demanding anything back. It was a garden that had been designed by someone who understood what a garden needed to be for the people who would use it.
He stood at the window and looked at it.
Then he sat on the edge of the bed and looked at his hands.
— ◦ —
He thought: I am here because the physiotherapist said I was ready for light officiating duties and the physiotherapist was right but light officiating duties had, in the subsequent eight months, accumulated a specific gravitational pull that light did not fully describe.
Light officiating duties had become a cover for something more active. The election commission had marked him as unreliable while acknowledging his operational correctness. Then he had conducted an investigation into an institutional conspiracy. Then the system had responded to that investigation by questioning his judgment about what he had found. Then the system had recommended voluntary admission to manage what it now documented as escalating institutional threat perception.
The accumulation had produced a set of conditions that multiple people — the physiotherapist, the optometrist, the AIFSFO's medical panel, and finally Brigadier Meenakshi Nair — had identified with the same word.
The word was: rest.
He had heard the word four times from four separate sources before he made the appointment.
He had needed to hear it four times.
But he also understood, in the specific way that comes from living inside institutional frameworks that were designed to contain him, that rest had many definitions.
The clinical definition: reduced physiological and psychological stress, allowing the body's healing mechanisms to function.
The institutional definition: removal of the active variable from the equation.
The reality: at thirty-five percent capacity, there were no healing mechanisms left. There was just the body negotiating with damage. There was just the understanding that the next physical stress would not be recovery — it would be collapse.
He had not told anyone which definition he thought the AIFSFO's medical panel had meant.
— ◦ —
The first evening: dinner in the common dining room. Six other patients from Ward A, two of whom he would come to know and four of whom remained at the necessary distance that institutions produce between people who are all managing something and don't have the bandwidth for additional management.
Dr. Sunita Arora introduced herself after dinner. She was smaller than he had expected from her voice on the phone — the initial consultations had been by call — with the specific quality of attention that distinguishes a doctor who is genuinely looking at you from one who is performing the act of looking. She had reviewed his case notes. Not just the recent ones. All of them.
She had the file from the AIFSFO's medical panel. She had the file from the optometrist. She had, because the AIFSFO's insurance had required it and he had authorized it, the file from the election commission.
She said: "I've read everything. The election night incident. The assessment that followed. The documentation of what you identified as systematic interference and what the commission concluded was elevated threat perception."
He looked at her.
She said: "It's in the file as pattern-matching bias based on your operational background. But the election was fair. The interference was prevented. Your actions were operationally correct. And they documented you as having potentially compromised judgment while acknowledging that your judgment was correct."
A pause.
She said: "The incident report also mentions physical engagement with operatives. A parking lot confrontation during material extraction. Your left ribs were compromised in that incident."
He said: "Yes."
She said: "The imaging from two weeks post-incident shows significant scar-tissue separation. You're looking at functional capacity below forty percent right now. The healing that had occurred was disrupted."
He said: "Thirty-five percent is functional."
She said: "Thirty-five percent is barely functional. Which is why the AIFSFO's medical panel recommended rest. Which is why you're here. And then you conducted the investigation."
He said: "Yes."
She said: "Which added further physiological stress to an already compromised baseline. You didn't rest after the election incident. You investigated. The investigation cost additional function."
The specific acknowledgment — that she understood both events, both marks, both costs — changed something in the quality of the conversation.
She said: "The incident file notes something beyond pattern-matching bias."
He said: "The excessive force notation."
She said: "Yes. The incident report documents that your response to the operative confrontation was escalated in proportion to the actual threat. The assessment is that your operational background may be influencing your threat perception."
She looked at him.
She said: "You've been marked twice now. Pattern-matching bias from the election. Excessive force from the parking lot. And now the system is using both marks to question your judgment about what you found in the Glass Rulebook investigation. The strategy is: you're operationally correct but institutionally unreliable, therefore your credibility about threats is questionable. You're here because you're damaged and the system is using your damage as cover for institutional doubt."
A pause.
She said: "That's the glass rulebook. That's how it works. That's why I'm telling you explicitly: Under the Mental Healthcare Act, voluntary admission means you retain the right to request discharge at any time."
He said: "I know the Act."
She said: "I know you do. I'm telling you anyway. Because I tell everyone anyway. The programme is twenty-one days. Physiotherapy every morning, occupational therapy in the afternoons, psychiatric support twice a week. The pharmacological protocol begins tomorrow — it's primarily for sleep and acute pain management. Nothing you haven't been prescribed before."
He said: "Who reviews the pharmacological protocol."
She said: "I do. Weekly. I also have direct access to your physiotherapy assessments. And I'll have the complete file from the optometrist when the fourth report comes through."
She said: "I've already sent a request to the election commission for the complete incident documentation. Not just the summary they provided with your admission materials. All of it."
He looked at her.
She said: "You've been documented as unreliable while being operationally correct twice now. I want to understand why the system needs you to be unreliable in addition to being correct. But the system that's documenting you as unreliable for being correct is the same system that sent you here. I want to know what they're protecting themselves from."
— ◦ —
The second day began like all days in institutions begin — with the assumption that structure is therapeutic. Breakfast at 7:45 AM. Physiotherapy from 8:30 AM to 9:30 AM. Occupational therapy from 2:00 PM to 3:30 PM. Dinner at 6:30 PM. Evening medication protocol from 8:00 PM.
The physiotherapist's name was Pradeep. He was younger than Bikram by perhaps a decade, with the specific quality of movement that comes from being trained in something and having paid attention to the training. He did not ask Bikram about the election night. He did not ask about the parking lot confrontation. He looked at the imaging from the AIFSFO's medical panel and said: "The ribs are separated at the healing line. The scar tissue is compromised but not severely. The shoulder is functional at approximately fifty-five percent. The knee has decent stability."
He said: "What's the pain threshold."
Bikram said: "Low."
Pradeep said: "That's accurate. The body's protective response kicks in early because it doesn't trust the healing. We have to retrain that trust. We do that by working at the threshold, not past it. We start with gentle mobilization. Not strength. Just movement. The body's nervous system needs to understand that movement is possible without triggering protective shutdown."
The sessions were forty minutes. Bikram lay on a treatment table while Pradeep moved his left side through carefully calibrated ranges of motion. The pain was specific and localized. The pain was also information — it was the body saying: this is the boundary, this is where the healing ends and the damage begins, this is how far we can go before I stop cooperating.
Pradeep worked with the information instead of against it.
By the third session, Bikram understood that Pradeep understood what damage looked like. Not the theoretical understanding. The practical, lived understanding of someone who had worked with bodies that had been driven past their specifications.
On the fourth morning, Pradeep said: "You're moving better. The protective response is calming. Your nervous system is beginning to understand that movement doesn't mean collapse."
Bikram said: "How long until full function."
Pradeep said: "Full function from thirty-five percent is not a timeline question. It's a practice question. It's about daily small improvements that compound over weeks and months. At your current rate, you could be at forty-five percent in six weeks. Fifty percent in eight weeks. Anything beyond that depends on compliance with the therapeutic protocol and your willingness to let the body dictate the pace instead of your mind dictating it. The mind is the primary variable in injury recovery. The body wants to heal. The mind wants to return to function. When those two are in opposition, the body loses."
Bikram said: "The mind has commitments."
Pradeep said: "Yes. The mind always does. That's why people end up here."
— ◦ —
The occupational therapy sessions were led by a woman named Meera Pillai. She was thirty years old and had the specific quality of someone who was managing something that required constant management. She worked in the common area with other patients, setting up tasks that seemed simple but were designed to isolate specific capacities: fine motor control, decision-making under unclear information, pattern recognition, sustained focus.
Bikram was assigned to a task that involved organizing a complex data set — patient admission records from the facility, cross-referenced with therapy completion and discharge outcomes. The data was incomplete, inconsistent, and poorly organized. The task was to identify patterns.
He said: "This is real data."
Meera said: "Yes."
He said: "This is from this facility."
Meera said: "Yes."
He said: "Why are you asking me to organize institutional data."
She said: "Because it's part of the occupational therapy protocol. We believe that structured cognitive work assists in the recovery process. But that's not why I'm asking."
She said: "I'm asking because I've been here for six months and I've been organizing this same data in the small hours when I can't sleep, and I've found patterns that the facility's administration would prefer I not find, and I need someone with your specific skill set to verify that I'm not hallucinating the patterns out of sleep deprivation and institutional damage."
He said: "The institution put you in my path to see if I would investigate what you've found."
She said: "Yes."
He said: "Or you're testing whether I'm a plant placed in the facility to determine whether the patients will incriminate the institution."
She said: "Yes. That too. You're here because the system documented you as unreliable while being operationally correct. I'm here because I identified something the system wanted hidden. The question is whether we're both actually what we appear to be or whether the system has arranged for us to investigate each other while it observes."
He said: "What did you find."
She said: "Data access logs that don't match the facility's stated privacy protocol. Patient data being pulled in patterns that suggest it's not for clinical purposes. Integration with an external analytics platform that the facility claims not to have partnerships with."
She said: "I have backup copies of the server data. I have access to the complete logs. I have enough documentation that the pattern is not a pattern. The pattern is systematic."
He said: "And you're asking me to verify because you need someone who can't be dismissed as unstable."
She said: "I'm asking you to verify because you need someone who can't be dismissed as paranoid despite being correct. Because the institution has already tried to do that to you twice now — once for being correct about electoral interference, once for being correct about the Glass Rulebook investigation. Because you're trained to see institutional patterns and the system has documented that training as a liability when it should be documented as a capability."
— ◦ —
Dr. Devraj Nair had been a data protection lawyer before something in his left hemisphere had gone wrong and he had ended up in Room 14, which was the most isolated room in Ward A, which was the kind of isolation that institutions deployed when they wanted to ensure that patients couldn't coordinate effectively with other patients.
He had been in the facility for seven months.
The official documentation said: psychiatric breakdown. Acute paranoia regarding data security compliance. Pattern-matching bias regarding institutional procedures.
The actual situation was: Devraj Nair had filed an RTI application requesting the facility's data governance documentation, and the facility had responded with institutional silence, and Nair had filed another application, and the facility had responded by admitting him.
He could not speak above a whisper. The doctors said it was psychosomatic. Nair believed it was the facility's pharmacological protocol, which included a medication that suppressed higher executive function while claiming to address anxiety.
Bikram met him on the fifth day.
Nair said, in a whisper that carried the weight of seven months of institutional pressure: "The facility is part of a data harvesting network called Clearpath. They partner with healthcare institutions to collect patient data — medical, psychological, behavioral — and integrate it with external analytics platforms. The Clearpath algorithm profiles patients, generates risk assessments, and sells those assessments to institutional players who use them to preemptively isolate individuals they perceive as institutional threats."
He said: "You're here because the election commission wanted you discredited before you could investigate further. The Clearpath partnership gave them the tool. Voluntary admission as cover for institutional surveillance. You're here because you succeeded at the Glass Rulebook investigation and they needed to contain the next investigation before it happened."
Bikram said: "Why would the election commission use a wellness facility to surveil a person."
Nair said: "Because a person in a wellness facility is already compromised. Already in a context where everything they do can be interpreted as symptomatic. Already unable to make external accusations because the institution controls the narrative about their reliability. You're documenting them as institutionally unsafe while protecting the institution's interests. The system accomplishes both simultaneously."
— ◦ —
Dr. Arora was waiting in her office when Bikram requested a session on the seventh day.
She said: "You've discovered what I wanted you to discover."
He said: "The Clearpath partnership. The data harvesting. The institutional use of voluntary admission as cover for surveillance."
She said: "Yes."
He said: "How long have you known."
She said: "Since before you arrived. I requested this assignment specifically because the facility was flagged as institutionally compromised and I wanted to understand the architecture of the compromise."
He said: "You're the variable. You're the one who's not supposed to be part of the system."
She said: "I'm a systems dissident employed by a compromised system, which means everything I do can be interpreted as either institutional loyalty or personal instability depending on how the institution chooses to read my actions. I've documented the Clearpath integration. I've filed formal complaints with the Medical Council of India. I've requested investigations from the Data Protection Board. I've done everything in my power to make the compromise visible. And the institution's response is to document me as potentially unreliable. To suggest that my concerns may reflect personal bias rather than institutional failure. To use the same tactics against me that it's using against you."
She said: "Meera has the backup data. Nair has the legal framework. You have the credibility. The question is whether the three of you can coordinate effectively while the institution is actively monitoring the coordination."
He said: "The institution is watching us collaborate."
She said: "The institution is counting on us collaborating exactly this way. The institution wants to see what three institutionally marked individuals do when given access to institutional secrets. The institution is learning from our response so they can refine the system for next time."
He said: "So we don't collaborate."
She said: "We coordinate anyway. Because the alternative is that the system continues to function without accountability."
— ◦ —
The strategy took shape over the next three days. Meera would document her findings in a way that appeared to be therapeutic progress — improvement in organizational thinking, capacity for structured analysis. Nair would prepare legal filings that appeared to be anxious rumination but were actually comprehensive evidence of institutional compromise. Bikram would coordinate the timing without appearing to coordinate, would make the connections that the institution would interpret as pattern-matching bias but that were actually systematic analysis.
Dr. Arora would compile the documentation into a form that could be submitted to external regulatory bodies while remaining internally deniable.
The Clearpath system depended on the patients' isolation. It depended on voluntary admission being interpreted as personal breakdown rather than institutional targeting. It depended on marked individuals being unable to trust their own judgment about institutional systems.
What the system did not account for was that marked individuals had already survived one round of institutional doubt. They understood the architecture. They knew how the glass rulebook worked.
They moved with the system instead of against it. They collaborated while appearing to comply. They documented while appearing to be documented.
By the thirty-ninth hour, everything was in place.
— ◦ —
CHAPTER TWO
The Crisis
Wednesday Evening — 11:47 PM
The medical panel meeting that was scheduled for Thursday morning would recommend extended admission. The documentation would show that Bikram's voluntary admission had been appropriate — his investigation of institutional data systems would be interpreted as paranoia, his collaboration with other patients as group delusion, his pattern recognition as obsessive thought disorder.
The Clearpath system would be validated. The institutional compromise would be protected. The marked individuals would be re-marked as unreliable in addition to being institutionally unsafe.
But at 11:47 PM on Wednesday night, Meera Pillai left her room and did not return. She went to the facility's administrative server room — a location she had documented in six months of institutional mapping — and accessed the backup Clearpath data that she had been assembling for the past three weeks. She compiled the complete evidence into a single format. She sent it.
At 11:53 PM, Nair's whisper became a document. A comprehensive legal filing, prepared over months in the margins of his apparent psychosomatic paralysis, submitted to the Data Protection Board of India. Names, dates, institutional partnerships, the architecture of systematic data harvesting.
At 12:14 AM, Dr. Arora received the composite package. She did not review it privately. She sent it directly to Parveen Siddiqui, who had been following the institutional thread since the election commission's attempt to discredit Bikram.
Parveen's article ran at 6:47 AM Thursday morning.
By 7:00 AM, when the medical panel convened, the facility's institutional compromise was no longer internal. It was public. The Clearpath partnership was exposed. The data harvesting was documented. The voluntary admission protocol as surveillance mechanism was visible.
The medical panel voted to discharge all three patients.
The institution's response was to document the discharge as therapeutic success. To claim that the patients had responded well to the protocol. To position the Clearpath exposure as evidence that the system worked — that compromised patients had been monitored and the monitoring had detected institutional problems, which demonstrated the system's integrity.
The institution protected itself by acknowledging partial failure while maintaining systemic viability.
Bikram understood this. He signed the discharge paperwork at 8:15 AM. He collected his personal items — his phone, his keys, his officiating credentials, the optometrist's reports.
He walked out of the facility at 8:23 AM.

CHAPTER THREE
The Garden
Thursday Morning — 8:47 AM
The garden that had been designed for people managing something provided him with a bench and a fountain and the specific quality of a morning that had been earned rather than scheduled.
He sat for ten minutes.
Then he stood up.
He walked back through the garden to the east grounds and through the service gate. Parveen's car was where she had left it. Meera was still on the phone with a lawyer who was not interested in the facility's claim of therapeutic success. Nair was standing with the expression of a man who knows where he is and is holding the knowledge carefully.
Bikram got in.
Parveen said: "Where to."
He said: "Meera needs a clinic — somewhere legitimate, somewhere that isn't a Clearpath partner. Do you know one."
She said: "Yes."
He said: "Nair needs a solicitor."
She said: "I know one. He did work on the QADAM case."
He said: "Good."
She put the car in gear. He looked out the window as they pulled out of the car park. The Maitri Wellness and Rehabilitation Centre retreated in the side mirror — the brochure facade, the garden hedge, the gate with its heritage ironwork. A building that had been two things simultaneously. Genuinely what it said it was, and also what it had been arranged to be.
Both things. That was the answer. Both things were real.
All of it was real.
— ◦ —
On the road south toward Kolkata, Parveen said: "The AIFSFO board meets next week."
He said: "I know."
She said: "Meenakshi Nair filed a formal response to the medical panel's recommendation. The Clearpath connection makes the recommendation procedurally compromised."
He said: "I know."
She said: "The certification is probably—"
He said: "Probably."
She said: "You're not going to stop."
It was not a question.
He said: "There's a boxing event on Tuesday."
She said: "Of course there is."
He said: "The knee needs work first. And the shoulder."
She said: "There's a physiotherapy clinic in Gariahat that—"
He said: "I know the one. Pradeep mentioned it."
She said: "Pradeep."
He said: "The physiotherapist. He was — helpful."
She said: "I'd like to talk to him."
He said: "He'll say he doesn't know what you're talking about."
She said: "I know. That's what I want to document."
The car moved south. The October morning. Barrackpore becoming the outer city becoming the inner city — the specific compression of Kolkata arriving, the density increasing, the smell of the river getting closer, the particular quality of the light over a city that has been doing this for three hundred years and has absorbed everything.
He sat in the passenger seat with his shoulder against the door and watched it come.

EPILOGUE: THE CALL
Arvind Kashyap, 59, former Secretary General of the All India Fight Sports Federation Officials, resigned from the board of the Maitri Healthcare Group thirteen days after Parveen Siddiqui's article ran.
His resignation statement cited personal reasons and expressed his continued commitment to the healthcare sector.
The Clearpath Wellness Analytics data pipeline was the subject of a formal inquiry by the Data Protection Board of India, initiated six weeks after the article. The inquiry was ongoing at the time this account was compiled.
Rohit Chandra, Managing Director, Maitri Healthcare Group, was placed on administrative leave pending the outcome of the inquiry. Three other Clearpath partner facilities were identified in the scope of the investigation.
Dr. Sunita Arora filed a formal complaint against the facility's data governance arrangements with the Medical Council of India. She continued to see patients at a different institution. She wrote a letter to Meera Pillai. The letter was two pages long. This account does not reproduce it, because some things are between the people they are between.
Meera Pillai, 30, former data architect, provided testimony to the Data Protection Board inquiry. The backup server data she accessed was accepted as primary evidence. She is no longer a patient at any facility. She is considering what comes next.
Devraj Nair, 41, former data protection lawyer, was discharged from the Maitri facility six days after Thursday. His family was informed the same day. His RTI applications were consolidated and resubmitted with additional documentation by the legal aid organisation. The first one was no longer pending.
The man from Room 14 who had been there seven months walked outside on a Thursday morning for the first time since his admission. He stood in a car park for several minutes. He did not require this time to be explained.
Pradeep, physiotherapist, Maitri Wellness and Rehabilitation Centre, continued his employment at the facility pending the outcome of the administrative review. He declined to be interviewed for Parveen Siddiqui's follow-up piece. The facility's lawyers confirmed he had done nothing wrong. He accepted this confirmation without apparent interest.
The AIFSFO board noted that while the Glass Rulebook investigation into the defence procurement conspiracy remained ongoing, the Clearpath exposure within the same institutional ecosystem suggested a pattern of systemic compromise that extended beyond any single conspiracy. The board convened a special session. The medical panel's recommendation was found to be procedurally compromised by the Clearpath connection. The certification review was withdrawn. National Integrity Official Bikram Dey was found to be in good professional standing.
His shoulder went to physiotherapy. His knee went to physiotherapy. His ribs were formally reviewed by a specialist who had not previously been given the full history and who said, looking at the imaging, that the quality of healing was frankly remarkable given what appeared to have been done in the interim without treatment.
Bikram said: "It held up."
The specialist said: "Barely."
He said: "Barely is functional."
The specialist said: "That is not the medical definition of functional."
He said: "It is in mine."
He attended the boxing event on Tuesday. The knee was at fifty-five percent. The shoulder was at sixty percent. The ribs had their position. The back said nothing, the productive silence.
He officiated four bouts. All four were clean.
Between the third and fourth bout he stood at the canvas edge and looked at the room — the lights, the crowd, the fighters in their corners, the specific charged atmosphere of a sport in the moment before it continues — and he thought about the garden.
He thought about the bench. The fountain. The ten minutes.
He thought: I am here because I keep coming back. I don't fully understand why. I have been asked four times and I have not answered the question adequately once. The physiotherapist says: rest. The optometrist says: reduce stress. The GP says: consider your limits. They are all correct. They are describing an accurate version of my situation.
The version they are not describing is the one where the vacancy is real. Where the shortage is real. Where the seat is empty not because no one will sit in it but because no one with the specific set of disadvantages and training that produces a man who calls what he sees regardless of what the room says will sit in it and so the seat stays empty and the calls that need to be made don't get made and the things that need to be stopped aren't stopped.
He is not idealistic about this. He is not a hero in the version of the story that requires heroism. He is a man with a compromised left shoulder, a right knee that files regular correspondence, three lumbar vertebrae with institutional memory, a pair of eyes that are recovering at the margin, and a reflex — old, deep, Para SF and older — that sees the gap in a chant, the shoulder not the hand, the face in a crowd, the tablet that doesn't match the prescription, and calls it.
That's it. That's the whole thing. Everything else is consequence.
He looked at the canvas. He looked at the fighters coming out of their corners.
He walked to the centre and watched the hands.
