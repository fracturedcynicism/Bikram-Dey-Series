THE GLASS RULEBOOK
 
A Novella
Being the fourth night of Bikram Dey
FRACTURED CYNICISM
"The glass rulebook: a set of rules everyone can see through
 and nobody is allowed to mention."
---
Fourteen months ago, Bikram Dey made three wrong calls at a football match and spent the night running through Kolkata.
Eight months ago, he made one correct call at a combat tournament and spent the night fighting through Kolkata.
Two months ago he was assigned to protect an election.
He succeeded. The system recorded the success.
The system also recorded something else.
Three weeks later, his physiotherapist told him he was ready.
He renewed his certification.
This story begins in Tangra.
It ends at a set of stone steps at dawn.
Between those two facts is this.

CHAPTER ONE
The Underground
Tangra, Kolkata — 9:15 PM
Tangra smells of tannery and rain and something that the tannery left behind when it closed and the rain cannot fully dissolve. It smells of the business of living done at close quarters across a very long time. Kolkata's Chinatown — what remains of it, the Chinese leather industry gone now, the restaurants staying because restaurants outlast everything — occupies the city's eastern margin with the stubborn permanence of a neighbourhood that has been written off repeatedly and not received the notification.
The Dongfang Club was not on any map. It occupied the basement of a building on a lane off Tangra Road that had, at street level, a functioning auto parts shop and, at first-floor level, the offices of a logistics company whose function Bikram had not examined closely enough to characterise. The basement had been, at various points in its history, a storage facility, a printing press, a mahjong room, and currently a combat sport event space with a twelve-by-twelve canvas mat, two hundred folding chairs, and a ventilation system that predated the current owners and had outlasted several sets of opinions about its adequacy.
Bikram had been here three times. The first two had been straightforward. Underground events in Kolkata occupied a specific legal grey, the kind that persists because the relevant authorities have reached a collective assessment that the available alternatives are worse, and the grey produces a functional ecosystem: fighters, corners, a small but consistent paying audience, and officials willing to work at the rate the ecosystem supports.
The rate was not good. He was not here for the rate.
He was here because the alternative was sitting in his guesthouse near Ultadanga reading the fourth optometrist's report for a fifth time, and he had read it enough times to know that reading it again would not change what it said, which was that his depth perception was recovering and that recovery was dependent on reduced physiological stress and regular therapeutic intervention and adequate rest.
He had been doing one of those three things.
The other two required not being at events like this one, which meant being at his guesthouse, which meant the report.
He had made a decision about the report. The decision was: later.
— ◦ —
Two bouts before the main event. He was watching from the canvas's edge, hands behind his back, reading the room the way he always read rooms now — exits, columns, the weight distribution of the crowd in the folding chairs, who was watching the fight and who was watching the doors.
The shoulder was at sixty percent. The knee had its position. The ribs — the left side, specifically, ribs six and seven — were something different now. The original fractures from Book Two had healed to a baseline. The scar tissue had held. Then the parking lot incident at the election had separated that healing. The tissue that was supposed to be load-bearing had been compromised. He carried this information the way he carried all information about his body: as a fact that required working around, not as a complaint.
The back said nothing. The back had learned a specific kind of silence.
The main event was a Muay Thai contest. Both fighters were good — real good, not curated-for-an-audience good. The promoter, a compact Hakka-Chinese man named Vincent Lo who had been running this room since 2017 and had never once in Bikram's experience given him any cause for concern, ran clean events. The informality of the venue did not extend to the sport.
Bikram watched the fighters.
He was also watching the crowd.
This was not strategy. It was reflex. Twelve years of operational deployment had made the peripheral register automatic — the part of his visual field that isn't where you're looking is still processing. It doesn't require instruction. It just does it. It files what it finds.
At the ninth minute of the second round, it filed something.
— ◦ —
The man was in the third row. Right side. Corner seat — the seat nearest the service stairs, the one that faces both the canvas and the room's eastern exit simultaneously. This was not a coincidence. You don't take that specific seat by coincidence. You take it because you've assessed the room and you want visibility in two directions.
Bikram had noticed the seat choice. Filed it. Continued watching the fight.
Then the man turned his head.
Forty-five degrees. Looking toward the eastern exit. Something at the exit had his attention. In the turn, the room's single overhead light — one industrial pendant, positioned above the canvas, creating a hard central pool and a graduated shadow toward the edges — caught his face at a specific angle for approximately one and a half seconds.
The face triggered something in Bikram's memory.
Not recognition exactly. Recognition is conscious. This was earlier than recognition — the pre-conscious filing that happens when the brain's pattern matching encounters something it has stored under a specific classification. The classification was: classified. The classification was also: documented.
The brain said: this is from a room you signed documents about.
The brain also said: this is from the election commission file.
He kept watching the fight. He did not look at the man again.
He needed seventeen seconds.
In seventeen seconds, the Para SF's operational memory — the specific faculty that is trained to retain faces, positions, operational details from briefings, the kind of retention that isn't general memory but targeted and durable — produced the file.
Commodore Ashok Mitra. Retired. Indian Navy. Last known position: Technical Advisory Committee, Ministry of Defence, 2019. Before that: Directorate of Naval Design. Before that: joint briefing, J&K, winter 2011. A classified room. A table with six people. Mitra had been there in an advisory capacity — naval intelligence assessment, cross-service. Bikram had been there because his unit had been tasked and needed the briefing.
He had signed documents about that room.
He had not thought about that room for eleven years.
The election commission file had listed Mitra as a probable vector in the interference attempt. Part of the operational network. Someone to watch.
Commodore Ashok Mitra had no visible reason to be in the basement of a building in Tangra watching an underground Muay Thai event on a Wednesday night.
Bikram kept his hands behind his back. He kept watching the fight. His face did not change.
Inside: the OODA loop initialising. Clean and fast, the way it does when the operational context is genuine.
Observe: face confirmed. Position confirmed. Seat choice: deliberate. His attention at 09:17 went to the eastern exit — someone was expected from that direction.
Orient: Mitra is not here for the fight. He's here for a meeting. The eastern exit leads to a service corridor and beyond that to a delivery lane on the building's north side. Private. Away from the street.
Decide: finish the bout. Do not engage. Do not look at him again. Exit through the west stairs after the bout, go east via the external lane, establish observation of the delivery lane before Mitra arrives at it.
Act.
The bout ran its last two minutes. Bikram called it clean. He raised the winner's hand. He spoke to Vincent Lo for the standard thirty seconds of post-bout formality. He moved to the west stairs.
Behind him, in the third row, the corner seat was already empty.
He moved faster.

CHAPTER TWO
The Lane
Tangra — 9:47 PM
The delivery lane on the building's north side was fourteen metres long, terminating in a gate that opened onto a secondary road running parallel to Tangra Road. It smelled of old leather — the ghost of the tanneries, embedded in the brick. Overhead the city's ambient light dissolved the stars completely. Two bare bulbs on the building's north face created overlapping pools of yellow. The gate was rusted iron, tall enough to be secure, old enough to have a gap at the hinge side that a man with specific experience would note.
Bikram was in the gap at the hinge side.
He had been there for ninety seconds when Mitra came out of the service door. Then a second man — not Mitra — who had come from the east exit. The meeting Mitra had been waiting for.
The second man: late thirties. Bengali, Bikram assessed from the limited physical markers available in bad light. Well-dressed in a way that was specifically calibrated to read as unremarkable — good fabric, conservative cut, nothing that would register to a casual observer as either wealth or its absence. A man who had learned how to not be looked at twice.
They stood. They spoke. Not long — four minutes, maybe five. Bikram caught fragments. The lane was narrow and sound carried in it with the efficiency of old brick construction but the fragments weren't enough for context. He caught: the name of a company — Meridian Technical Solutions, repeated twice, once by each man. He caught a date — December, unspecified year. He caught the English phrase — odd in a Bengali conversation, which meant it was a term with a specific technical meaning that they hadn't bothered translating — acceptance testing.
Then the second man passed something. Small. Flat. An envelope, the size of a mobile phone, passed with the specific motion of someone who has done this before: no preamble, no ceremony, the transaction absorbed into the movement so that if you weren't watching for it you would not see the moment of transfer.
Bikram was watching for it.
He had it. He had the face. He had Meridian Technical Solutions. He had December. He had acceptance testing. He had the transaction.
He did not have enough. Not yet.
He had enough to know that he was going to need considerably more.
— ◦ —
Mitra went back through the service door. The second man went through the gate.
Bikram waited three minutes. Then he went through the gate.
His knee, sitting in the confined back seat of an auto-rickshaw three minutes later, registered its standard complaint about confinement. He noted it. He did not negotiate.
The secondary road was empty at this hour except for a parked tempo and a tea stall closing. He walked to Tangra Road, found an auto, gave his guesthouse address, sat in the back with his shoulder against the auto's side and watched the city move past and ran through everything he had just acquired.
He thought about the 2011 briefing. What he knew about Mitra from that room: naval intelligence background, attached to a cross-service assessment of a specific operational scenario that Bikram had been read into for twenty-two hours and then read out of. Mitra had been calm, technical, precise. A man who operated through institutional channels with confidence — the specific confidence of someone whose power comes from systems they trust to sustain them.
What Bikram knew about Mitra from the election commission file: probable participant in the interference attempt. Contact with institutional actors. Listed as "person of interest" in the systematic approach to election night polling stations.
What Bikram knew from the last eight weeks: that being a person of interest in an institutional context was a category that included himself.
That the system documented you as being correct about a threat while also documenting that your judgment about threats was potentially compromised by pattern-matching bias.
That the system could hold both truths simultaneously and use the contradiction as a tool.
— ◦ —
He spent Wednesday night researching. Meridian Technical Solutions was registered as an import-export firm with offices in Ballyganj and an online presence that amounted to: a website with no meaningful content, a registered phone number that did not answer, an email address that did not respond. Standard cover. The company existed for the purpose of not being looked at.
His ribs said something. He registered it. Continued.
The company's filing history: established 2019, registered to a person named Vikram Sharma, currently listed as Managing Director. Vikram Sharma did not have a public presence — no social media, no professional listings, no visible biography. A person successfully learning how to not be looked at.
Sixth search: naval turbine import contract India 2024.
The search produced results, but not the kind that made obvious narrative sense. There were press releases about a tender for naval propulsion components. There were obscure engineering papers about turbine efficiency in saltwater. There was a government announcement about a revised timeline for the domestic naval propulsion programme, pushed back six months. There was mention of "quality assurance delays" and "upgraded acceptance testing requirements."
The fragments were there. Meridian. Acceptance testing. A timeline. A defence project. The pieces did not fit together but they fit adjacent to each other like tiles that were almost matching.
His knee had the same view as always. His shoulder was maintaining its position. The back was saying nothing, which was either good news or sophisticated strategy.
He had the shape of it. Not the proof. The shape.
He closed the laptop. He lay down on the bed. He stared at the ceiling.
He thought: I am two months out of WARD. I have a recovering depth perception and a medical report that says the recovery depends on reduced physiological stress. I also have a system that has documented me as having pattern-matching bias in threat assessment, which means that whatever shape I'm seeing, the system will interpret my seeing it as another example of the problem they've already identified. I understand this and I am proceeding anyway because the alternative is that the pattern continues undetected.
— ◦ —
By Thursday evening, he had the institutional framework.
The domestic naval surveillance and targeting programme — NLST — had been developed over fifteen years with the goal of upgrading India's naval surface-to-air capability. The programme had three phases: design, development, and deployment. The deployment phase required acceptance testing of turbine components. The acceptance testing had to be done by vendors who were not connected to the primary contractors, which meant external partners, which meant import relationships, which meant companies like Meridian Technical Solutions.
The shape was becoming visible.
Mitra's institutional position: Technical Advisory Committee. The Committee reviewed major defence procurement. Mitra would have visibility into the entire programme architecture. Mitra would also have access to testing schedules, vendor requirements, and approval protocols.
What someone might do with that visibility: redirect the programme's vendor testing to someone they controlled. Someone like Vikram Sharma at Meridian Technical Solutions.
What someone might do with that control: certify components that were not actually up to specification. Create a window in the system where defective parts could move through approved channels. Sell the approved-but-defective components at a premium to buyers who trusted the approval. Repeat this across multiple vendors, multiple timelines, multiple systems.
The scale would be extraordinary. The scale would be institutional.
He sat back from the laptop on the second evening. His eyes were tired. The fourth report said reduced physiological stress. The fourth report was in his pocket. He had not looked at it since WARD and was not looking at it now.
He thought about what he had.
The shape. The institutional pattern. The names and dates and program timelines.
What he did not have: a minute book. The Dongfang Club meeting had been about something specific. The envelope Mitra received had contained something specific. That something was the documentation that would connect Mitra's position to Meridian's operations.
That documentation was probably at the club. In Vincent Lo's office or files or security records.
Taking it would be institutional breaking and entering.
Not taking it meant proceeding on inference and pattern-matching, which meant proceeding exactly the way the system had documented him as being unreliable about proceeding.
He had made this decision before. He understood what it cost. He understood what happened when he made it anyway.
He stood up.

CHAPTER THREE
The Investigation
Friday — Waterfront Road, 4:22 AM
He walked along the waterfront road. His knee had opinions about the uneven surface. He walked anyway.
Beside the active facility: the retired docks. Berths that had not held a vessel in years. Sheds of corrugated iron, oxidised to the colour of old blood. The specific geography of industrial decommissioning — not abandoned exactly, not demolished, just present in the way of things that are no longer needed and have not yet been replaced by whatever comes next.
He was here because of a name in the research: Dr. Rajan Ghosh, 54, former project director of the domestic turbine programme's Kolkata component. The NLST had its primary facility in Vizag, but the programme had a secondary design office at Garden Reach — the proximity to the shipbuilders, the existing infrastructure, the institutional knowledge base. Dr. Ghosh had run that office from 2017 to 2023, when the programme was paused.
Dr. Ghosh was not listed anywhere. No academic papers post-2023. No professional consulting work. No public record of what he was doing with the life he had after the programme paused.
What Bikram had found: Dr. Ghosh owned a small property near the retired docks. A warehouse of approximately 2,000 square feet. The warehouse was registered to him personally. The property had been quiet for approximately eighteen months.
Quiet was interesting.
Quiet was worth a walk at 4:22 in the morning.
The warehouse was behind a gate. The gate was locked but the lock was simple and the chain holding it was old. Bikram stood outside for three minutes, running the logistics. Breaking the chain would take four seconds. Securing the chain behind him so that casual observation would not register the break would take another twelve seconds. Clearing the warehouse interior to confirm whether anyone was inside would take four minutes.
His ribs were communicating. He had been crouching in front of filing cabinets for forty-one minutes and the ribs had a comprehensive account of what that had cost. He had found the minute book. He had found correspondence. He had found the diagram that connected Mitra to Meridian, that connected Meridian to the NLST programme, that connected the programme's approval process to a systematic replacement of components with non-specification parts.
He had found institutional conspiracy.
Today was Wednesday. He had tonight and tomorrow.
He stood. He breathed.
— ◦ —
The shoulder said something about the day's activity. He registered it. Walked back to the main road. Found a tea stall. Ordered tea. Drank it standing, looking at the club's front facade.
He thought: I am a sports official with a recovering depth perception and three historic vertebral fractures and a physiotherapist who told me eight weeks ago that I was progressing but that recovery required continued therapeutic protocol and reduced operational stress.
I am also a man who has just broken into a warehouse, photographed institutional documentation of a defence procurement conspiracy, and is now standing outside a tea stall where I have been ordered, on three separate occasions, to cease the specific activities that are keeping me at operational capacity.
Light officiating duties.
That was what the certification said.
Light officiating duties.
— ◦ —
The body inventory, Friday edition:
Right knee: present. The knee was a negotiation. It would hold at walking pace. It would hold on stairs if he used the rail. It would not hold at a full sprint and he was not planning a full sprint.
Left shoulder: sixty percent, stable. Do not use in the first thirty seconds of any engagement. Avoid overhead. The range limitation is not pain — it is structural. The tendons have healed to a new alignment that functions but does not function in the way the original alignment did. He knew this and worked with it.

In a low-light environment — a club's billiards room at evening, curtained windows, ambient lamp — the depth degradation would make close-distance judgement unreliable at anything over three metres. He sat in the dark writing room with this.
His shoulder said: present.
His knee said: present.
The ribs said: still here. Filing the documentation.
His back said nothing. His back was thinking.

Saturday evening. The Dongfang Club.
He had confirmed: Vincent Lo was expecting him. The official certification was current. The bouts were scheduled. His presence on the card meant the system had no reason to suspect that his primary business with the club had nothing to do with the fighting.
Vincent would not ask. Vincent understood the specific geometry of institutional grey — the space where people operated with minimal oversight because the oversight was expensive and the oversight's absence had not yet produced enough chaos to justify the cost of restoration.
Bikram changed into the official's uniform. He reviewed the bout cards. He checked the weight compliance. He did the standard procedures.
At 8:47 PM, the first bout was forty-two minutes away.
He was in the club's kitchen — the actual kitchen, where Vincent prepared tea for the officials — when the man in the conservatory space adjacent said something that the system would, later, interpret as very much not fine.
"I need to speak with you."
His eyes, in the billiards room's lamp-and-ambient light, were operating at about seventy percent. At the room's dimensions — twelve metres by eight — everything within four metres was reliable. Beyond that: functional but not fine.
This was the room. These were the men.
The man in the conservatory space was Vikram Sharma. The man beside him was someone Bikram had not filed before — operational, clearly, but unregistered in his memory. The third man was someone whose photograph Bikram had seen in the warehouse documents. Someone named Krishnamurthy. Someone involved in the component substitution at the acceptance testing level.
The minute book was on the desk to Krishnamurthy's right.
"You have the documentation," Sharma said. It was not a question.
Bikram said: "Yes."
"You need to understand what you're interfering with," Krishnamurthy said. "This is not a local operation. This is institutional. This has protections."
The registered man — Bikram thought him as "Hora," because the brain assigns names to unknown faces and Hora seemed approximately correct — moved toward Bikram. Not rushing. Professional. Establishing proximity as a form of communication.
Bikram was standing against the kitchen counter. The kitchen counter had a specific advantage: it was solid at his back. No surprises from behind. Limited directions of approach. The registered man was moving from the left. Krishnamurthy was at the table, civilian, not going to advance unless the registered man established that approach was warranted.
This was the strategic moment. Before physical escalation. Before the system interpreted any response as "pattern-matching bias in threat assessment, leading to escalation in proportion to perceived rather than actual danger."

Any action you take against us looks like confirmation of the documentation."
This was accurate.
This was also the moment Hora committed.
The registered man moved from two metres to contact distance in approximately one second. His approach was technically sound — straight lines, minimal telegraphing, weight transfer that was balanced for following through or stopping depending on response. He threw a directed strike, hand rather than full commitment, designed to open the distance and establish dominance.
Bikram was against the counter, which meant limited backwards retreat. Which meant forward response.
Para SF close-quarters framework is not about stopping force. It's about borrowing force and returning it at an angle the original source didn't plan for.
Bikram pivoted his hips. The registered man's forward momentum, redirected against the hip, produced a half-rotation that put the man's back against the kitchen's brick wall in a way that the wall registered as solid and persuasive.
Hora made a sound. The sound was the sound of a large man making contact with old brick at velocity.
His ribs filed an emergency motion about what that redirection had cost. He noted it. Continued.
Mitra had moved toward the billiards table — not toward Bikram, away from him, putting the table between them, which was a correct instinct that also put him at distance. Krishnamurthy had not moved. Krishnamurthy was a civilian who had spent his career at desks and the desk reflex — stay, assess, respond later — was still operating.
The registered man was recovering, which meant Bikram needed to create distance.
He walked. Then he ran.
His knee's opinion about running was immediate and comprehensive and he ran anyway, the specific Para SF run that is not a sprint but a managed pace that trades speed for duration and is calibrated to put distance between positions.
He went back through the connecting door. Writing room, corridor, service stairs, service corridor, eastern service door. The door opened onto the lane. The lane was dark. The lane was clear.

Parveen.

He was not the man he had been on football night. He was also not the man from the cold room in Book Two. CHAPTER FOUR
The Ghat
Sunday morning — 4:47 AM

His back said nothing. His back was thinking.
He stayed on the steps until 2 AM, which was when the first chai stall of the morning opened on the ghat road, the same stall he had sat at after the flower market, the same stall that had existed for longer than most reasons for sitting at it, and he walked over and sat at the counter and the same man — of indeterminate age, of unlimited capacity for the night's business, of zero interest in explanation — placed a glass on the counter and began to pour.
He breathed again.
He drank the tea. He watched the river.
At 4:00 AM, his phone rang again. Parveen.
"They've launched the inquiry," she said. "Full institutional review. The CBI is taking it seriously."
"And?" he said.
"And they're also launching a parallel review into the credibility of the sources. How the documentation was obtained. Whether the chain of custody was compromised."
"Expected," he said.
"They're not questioning the documentation," she said. "They're questioning you."
"Also expected," he said.
She said: "Don't thank me. Go see the physiotherapist."
He said: "Thank you."
He said: "Next week."
— ◦ —
Sunday evening. The Dongfang Club.
He stood up. His body conducted its standard morning assessment. The shoulder: present, sore, within operational parameters. The knee: post-operational, communicating, functional. The ribs: the emergency petition had resolved into a standard filing. The back: still saying nothing. He chose to receive the silence as good news.
He picked up his phone. He looked at it.
The fourth optometrist's report was in his pocket. He took it out. He looked at the sky. Then he looked at the report.
Recovering. That was the word. Not recovered. Recovering.
He walked back to the Dongfang Club. Vincent was waiting. The evening's schedule was unchanged. The bouts were beginning at 8:30 PM. The main event was set for 10:15 PM.
He changed into the uniform. He reviewed the bout cards. He checked the weight compliance. He did the standard procedures.
The first bout at 8:47 PM. He watched the fighters. He read the room. His depth perception, at the officiating range that mattered — two metres, the range where he could see the contact, the moment of incapacity, the instant when the system said: stop — was sufficient.
His shoulder maintained its position throughout. His knee participated without formal objection. His ribs were present and operational. The depth perception, at close officiating range — two metres, the range that matters — was sufficient.
He called four bouts. All four were clean.
After the last bout, he stood in the centre of the ring for a moment before stepping off. The arena was emptying. The overhead lights were coming down to maintenance level. The canvas was the canvas.
He thought about the glass rulebook. Visible to everyone. Untouchable by everyone who plays by it.
He changed out of the uniform in the changing room. Every movement was deliberate. The shoulder carried the cost of the week. The ribs were filing their standard petition with added emphasis. The knee was maintaining its position through what seemed to be sheer institutional persistence.
Colonel Ghosh was waiting in the corridor.
"We need to talk," Ghosh said.
They sat in Ghosh's office. Ghosh had documents in front of him — the AIFSFO's medical panel assessment of the incident file. The election night documentation. The Hastings Club confrontation. The physical engagement with the registered operative. The rib compromise documented, quantified, filed.
Ghosh said: "The board recommends voluntary admission to a wellness facility. Twenty-one days. Physiotherapy, psychiatric assessment, stress management protocol."
Bikram said: "That's institutional language for institutional doubt."
Ghosh said: "Yes. It's also accurate. You're at thirty-five percent baseline from the last incident. You've spent the week operating at that baseline while adding investigative stress. The physiological markers show degradation that requires either operational cessation or managed recovery protocol."
Bikram said: "And if I don't comply."
Ghosh said: "The certification gets reviewed by the full board. The pattern emerges: election night incident with dual institutional notation, Glass Rulebook investigation, Hastings Club physical engagement, refusal of medical recommendation. The system documents you as unreliable in your own self-assessment. The certification becomes permanently questionable."
Bikram understood this. The glass rulebook operating exactly as it was designed to operate.
Ghosh said: "Maitri Wellness Centre in Barrackpore. Twenty-one days. Voluntary admission means you have discharge rights at any time. But the appearance of cooperation matters to the board's perception of your judgment."
Bikram said: "The appearance."
Ghosh said: "The system needs the appearance more than it needs the reality."
Bikram said: "I'll do it."
Ghosh said: "I know you will. I'll notify the facility. Tuesday. Three PM. Bring minimal documentation. Your phone gets surrendered on intake."
Bikram stood. Ghosh extended his hand. The handshake was specific — acknowledgment, not congratulation. The recognition of a particular kind of person by another person who understood what cooperation cost when the system had already marked you as unreliable.
— ◦ —
He went back to his guesthouse. He called Parveen.
"I'm going to WARD," he said. "Twenty-one days. Voluntary admission."
"Why," she said.
"Because the system recommended it and the system's recommendation is how the system manages people it's marked."
"Are you in danger," she said.
"Probably," he said. "But the danger of refusing is greater. The refusal becomes confirmation of the unreliability they've already documented."
He made the appointment that night. Tuesday morning he packed minimal items — clothes, medications, documents the facility would require. The fourth optometrist's report went into his jacket pocket. It stayed there.
Tuesday afternoon at 3:00 PM he presented at Maitri Wellness and Rehabilitation Centre in Barrackpore with the paperwork Colonel Ghosh had prepared. Voluntary admission. No external coercion documented. The appearance of a man making a choice about his own health.
The intake process took forty-seven minutes. Forms, photographs, brief assessment. The surrender of his phone. The recording of his credentials. The institutional protocols.
He was shown to Room 7, Ward A, first floor.
By evening he was lying on the bed looking at a garden through the window — a garden designed for people managing something, planted with softness and non-sharpness, the specific institutional care given to spaces where people come to be helped or to be monitored or to be both simultaneously.
He understood what he was walking into.
He also understood that the alternative was standing still while the system continued — the pattern, the glass rulebook, the institutional protection of institutional actors while marking people who had attempted to expose them as unreliable.
The night arrived.
In Room 7, Ward A, at Maitri Wellness Centre, Bikram Dey lay in the dark and waited for whatever came next, understanding that the call had been made, that the minute book was in the system, that the turbine conspiracy was now in the hands of institutional process, and that this — voluntary admission, the appearance of cooperation, the surrender of his phone and his credentials — was the cost of being correct about what the system did to people who insisted on transparency.
He lay there and waited.
