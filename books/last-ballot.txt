A Novella
Being the third night of Bikram Dey
FRACTURED CYNICISM

The one that cannot be undone.
The one that, once cast, requires protection."

PART ONE: THE ASSIGNMENT
Six Weeks Ago — Physiotherapy Office, Park Street

The physiotherapy office smelled like the specific combination of heat and eucalyptus that all physiotherapy offices smell like, as if the therapeutic effect requires aesthetic endorsement. The smell had been there since 9 AM. It was now 2:47 PM. The smell was embedded in everything — the white walls, the examination table, the elastic bands hanging from the equipment rack. Bikram had been sitting in the waiting room for forty-three minutes while Dr. Pema Wangmo reviewed X-rays of his shoulder, his knee, his left ribs.
The ribs were the problem. The ribs had been the problem since the cold room in Book Two, when he had been held against a concrete floor while people who wanted information applied pressure in very specific ways. The ribs had never been formally treated. They had been assessed by a doctor who had bandaged them and told him to rest, and he had rested for approximately six days before the No Contest incident had required him to not rest anymore, and after that the ribs had learned to exist with the damage rather than process it toward healing.
Now, six months later, they were still learning.
Dr. Wangmo had her back to him, studying the light box. Her shoulders were the posture of someone reading news they had anticipated but hoped wouldn't be confirmed. She was fifty-two. She had been doing this for thirty years. She knew what healed and what did not. More importantly, she knew what healed if the patient stopped asking it to do more than it was capable of.
She turned around.
"You're at forty-five percent," she said.
What she meant: you've recovered enough that the next assignment will degrade you faster than the previous one.
Bikram was sitting on the examination table. His feet didn't touch the floor. The position put him at eye level with Dr. Wangmo, which was deliberate — examination tables were designed to position patients in a vulnerable stance. He was acutely aware of this.
He said: "What specifically are we talking about."
She pulled up a chair and sat across from him. Professional distance. She had already given him the verdict. Now she was explaining it.
"The left shoulder has recovered to approximately sixty percent of pre-injury capacity. The depth perception has improved to sixty-five percent. The knee is stable at around eighty percent because the knee injury was never severe — it was impact and contusion, not structural. The back — the three vertebrae — those are holding at baseline. No further degradation, which is good."
She paused.
"The ribs are the limiting factor. The left ribs six and seven — the fractures from the cold room — those are healed at approximately seventy percent. But seventy percent healed is not seventy percent functional. The healing left scar tissue. The scar tissue is less flexible than the original bone. The functional capacity is closer to fifty percent."
She said: "If you stress those ribs again — if you subject them to sustained pressure, to impact, to the kind of torso engagement required in combat sports — they will re-fracture. Not might. Will."
Bikram looked at her.
"The question is not whether you can recover further," she said. "The question is whether you're willing to limit your activities while that recovery happens."
"How long," he said.
"Three months minimum. Possibly four. With compliance."
Compliance. The word implied choice. The word required him to pretend that his body was not already making the decisions it was making, that rest was something he could sustain for three months when the past six months had been a negotiation with the concept of rest.
She stood up.
"Light officiating," she said. "Thirty-minute assignments. Nothing that requires sustained torso engagement. Nothing that requires full-weight transfer to the left leg. Nothing that requires overhead movement with the right shoulder."
She was describing a life that existed in theory.
"Football refereeing," she continued. "That's the limit. Football. Nothing with repeated striking, nothing with grappling, nothing with sustained defensive positioning."
She said: "The human body is not a machine. It can't be repaired to perfect specification. It can only be supported while it repairs itself. And it can only repair itself if it's not being asked to do the thing that damaged it in the first place."
He understood this. Understanding and accepting were different things.
"If you don't comply," she said, "the healing timeline extends. The next injury will cost more. You understand this."
He understood it. He understood that understanding was something he did very well and that accepting what he understood was a different skill entirely.
He was discharged from physiotherapy with a certificate that said he was ready for light officiating duties. The certificate was dated and signed. It was official. It was also a document that assumed he would do exactly what it told him to do, which was an assumption that had been incorrect every time anyone had made it.
He renewed his AIFSFO certification three days later. He told Colonel Ghosh he was operational.
Colonel Ghosh, who had been watching Bikram closely since the No Contest incident, said: "You look like you're held together by intention."
He said: "That's accurate."
Colonel Ghosh said: "For four weeks, you're football only. Nothing with sustained torso stress. Nothing with combat sports. Football. If I find out you've taken any other assignment, the certification gets reviewed."
He said: "Understood."
Colonel Ghosh said: "I mean it. The AIFSFO doesn't need another official whose body fails in the middle of an assignment. Especially not one I authorized despite medical restrictions."
He said: "I understand."
What he did not say: understanding and compliance were still different things. What he did not say: his body was going to do what bodies do, which was negotiate with pain and damage and the specific weight of continuing to exist despite both. What he did not say: he had spent enough time sitting with the limitations. It was time to stand up.

Four Weeks Later — Tea Stall, Ultadanga
The tea stall near Ultadanga charged whatever price felt appropriate based on the stall owner's assessment of the customer and the season and possibly the alignment of planets. The stall was chronically empty because it was located in an area that was difficult to find unless you knew about it, which meant the people who went there had gone there deliberately. Parveen Siddiqui was there deliberately at 4:02 PM on a Tuesday in the week before the municipal elections.
She had coffee, which was unusual. Kolkata was a tea city. Coffee was what people drank when they wanted to signal that they were outsiders or that they wanted to seem more formal than the tea would allow. Parveen's coffee came in a white cup with a handle. Her intensity came across as professional research, which it was.
She said: "Two months ago, I started tracking election-night irregularities. Not the normal ones — the shifting of votes, the miscounting, the administrative pressure. The systematic ones. The coordinated ones."
She showed him the USB drive. No copies. Just the drive. The contents were photographs of documents, timelines, patterns. She had been researching election interference for two years, she said. This was different.
"Booth 42," she said. "North Kolkata. Municipal election. Three council seats depend on the count from that booth. Two district committee positions. The mayoral race dynamics for the entire region."
She said: "Someone wants that booth to produce a specific result badly enough to apply organized pressure to make it happen."
Bikram looked at the tea stall owner. The stall owner was not listening. The stall owner had a specific skill for not listening to conversations happening at the table where he was not currently standing.
Bikram said: "What kind of pressure."
She said: "The kind that requires training. The kind that requires resources. The kind that has multiple vectors — administrative, operational, institutional. The kind that is designed to work through the system's own mechanisms rather than against them."
She said: "I've documented it across the past six weeks. Collection points compromised. Poll workers intimidated. The specific booths that matter under systematic pressure."
She said: "The election commission knows this is happening. They don't have the personnel to protect the booths. They're understaffed on election night. They need someone in Booth 42. Someone who can't be intimidated. Someone who understands protocol under pressure."
He looked at her.
She said: "I know what this costs you. I know the physiotherapist said light duties. I know the AIFSFO has restrictions."
He said: "It's not about cost."
She said: "Then what is it about."
He said: "It's about whether the system is still the system or whether it's become something else. Whether the rules still mean what they said they meant. Whether protocol is still something that works."
She looked at him across the plastic table for a long moment.
She said: "Yes. That's exactly it."
She said: "The rules still mean something. But only if someone enforces them when pressure is applied. Only if someone invokes them when the system tries to override them."
She said: "I need you to be that someone."

The Decision — Guesthouse, Ultadanga, 11:23 PM
He said yes.

PART TWO: THE NIGHT
Election Night — 6:47 PM
The evidence had to move before the operatives realized it was mobile. This was the thing Parveen said while they were sitting in the car two blocks from the collection center where she had been monitoring from a distance for the past week. She had documentation. She had names. She had the systematic proof of interference layered across multiple booths, but Booth 42 was the one that mattered because Booth 42 was where the interference would be visible in real time if someone was watching for it.
The drive was in a sealed envelope in a courier bag. The bag was in Parveen's car. The car was three kilometers from the collection center and the operatives who had been watching the center for the past twelve hours were not sophisticated about surveillance — they were functional, which meant they were good enough to track a moving target if the target was obvious.
Bikram said: "They'll move on the courier run."
Parveen said: "Yes. That's the actual vector. They're going to intercept the drive somewhere between here and the election commission office."
Bikram said: "Or they'll take out the vehicle and search it."
Parveen said: "Yes."
Bikram said: "So we don't use a courier run."
Parveen said: "How do we move it."
Bikram looked at the collection center. The operatives were inside. The drive was in his hands now — she had given it to him three minutes ago when they had driven past, a handoff so clean that the operatives had not registered the transition. This was the thing they were trained for. This was the reason he had been specified for this task.
He said: "We split the payload. You drive the courier vehicle exactly on schedule from the collection center. You take the backup documentation. The actual drive goes a different route."
Parveen said: "Where."
Bikram said: "Directly to the booth. Booth 42. It comes in with the official documentation that I'm supposed to review before the count starts. The operatives are expecting a courier run from here to the commission office. They're not expecting the drive to be at the booth."
Parveen said: "They'll figure it out."
Bikram said: "Probably. But not until after the count starts. And if the count starts without interference, the drive is already been reviewed and documented as part of the official record. They can't suppress evidence that's already part of the election documentation."
He said: "You take the courier vehicle. You drive directly. Don't deviate. Make them commit to the interception. Make them show their hand on camera — there are traffic cameras on the arterial routes. Let them try to take the decoy. It gets them on record."
Parveen said: "And you."
Bikram said: "I get the drive to Booth 42. I secure it. I document it. I protect the count."
He said: "By 7:15 PM, the election officially starts. By 8:00 PM, they'll realize they've been misdirected. By then, the count is in progress and the evidence is part of the official record."
The drive was a small thing. The weight of it in his jacket pocket was insignificant. The weight of what it contained was the kind of thing that didn't have a weight until the moment someone tried to take it from you.
Parveen started the car.

7:02 PM — Arterial Route, North Kolkata
The first indication that the operatives had committed to the interception was the motorcycle that appeared in her mirror at 7:02 PM. One vehicle, hanging back three car-lengths. That was technique. That was not parking enforcement or a random follower. That was coordinated surveillance that was about to become something more active.
Parveen kept the speed steady. She kept the route exactly as she had described to Bikram. The arterial route was monitored but not heavily — it was main road, commercial traffic, pedestrians during this hour despite the evening. The motorcycle maintained distance. She watched it in the mirror.
At 7:06 PM, a second vehicle appeared. A sedan. Unmarked but functional. This was the moment when the surveillance shifted from observation to commitment. Two vectors. One to track, one to move. This was how coordinated interception worked.
The sedan moved to her left. The motorcycle stayed behind. Tactical box forming — one side, one rear, the front still open because the front was where she was going to accelerate toward, which was exactly what the operatives expected her to do.
Except Bikram had told her not to do that.
At 7:07 PM, the traffic light ahead turned red. Parveen maintained speed. The intersection was a major crossing. Rush hour was beginning. There were vehicles in the perpendicular lanes. There was pedestrian traffic. There was the exact combination of civilian activity that made direct contact problematic.
Parveen accelerated through the red light.
The sedan accelerated with her. The motorcycle accelerated. Civilian vehicles swerved. A pedestrian stepped back from the curb. The intersection became a point of collision that did not quite happen but very nearly did, which was the whole architecture — near-collision as cover, tactical movement hidden behind the specific energy of traffic chaos.
The sedan pulled alongside. The passenger window rolled down. There was no weapon visible, but the hand that was visible on the door was stable and deliberate. This was technique. This was the message: we are here, we are coordinated, and you need to cooperate.
Parveen did not cooperate. She dropped her right-side mirror against the sedan's left side with enough force that the impact registered. Once. Not a collision — a contact. A message back.
The sedan pulled back. The driver had not expected impact. The driver had expected compliance or at least evasion, not active resistance. The psychological weight of unexpected violence was enough to cause a moment of recalibration.
That moment was enough.
Parveen took the next turn hard left, tires protesting the force, the courier vehicle not built for aggressive handling but built well enough to take it. The sedan tried to follow. The motorcycle tried to follow. The traffic on the perpendicular street was thicker and denser, and the taxi that Parveen had watched position itself two minutes ago for exactly this purpose pulled into the space between the sedan and her vehicle with the specific coordination that happens when there are two people talking on untraceable phones less than a kilometer apart.
The sedan had to brake or collide with the taxi. The motorcycle had to slow to avoid the same collision. Parveen kept moving.
By 7:09 PM, she had three kilometers' worth of urban infrastructure between herself and the interception vector. The backup documentation was safe. The courier vehicle was intact. The drive that Parveen did not have was supposed to be in her vehicle, but it was not. It was in Bikram's jacket pocket, moving toward Booth 42 by a different route.
At 7:11 PM, Parveen pulled into the election commission office parking area. The backup documentation went directly to the commission's records officer. The official receipt was timestamped and filed. No one asked about the USB drive because the USB drive was supposed to be in the courier documentation, and the documentation had been delivered.
At 7:12 PM, the operatives realized the drive was not in the vehicle and was no longer in Parveen's hands.
By that time, Bikram was six minutes out from Booth 42.

7:18 PM — Booth 42, North Kolkata
The booth was a secondary school classroom converted for election-day use. The poll workers were nervous. They had been told there might be pressure. They had not been told that the pressure would be this specific, this coordinated, or this willing to manifest as direct operational presence.
Bikram arrived at 7:18 PM with the official documentation and the sealed envelope. The poll workers knew him by sight — the AIFSFO had made his assignment official through the commission. They stood when he entered. The envelope went into the official locked box that would remain with the ballot materials. Procedure was procedure.
He said: "From 7:20 PM until the count completes, no one enters or leaves this booth except the poll workers and myself. The ballots remain under observation at all times. The count follows protocol exactly. No expedited timelines. No variation. If someone attempts entry, you contact the commission immediately and I document the attempt."
The poll workers said they understood.
Bikram said: "Someone will try to enter. They will have reasons that sound official. They will have pressure that sounds institutional. None of that matters. Protocol is what matters."
He said: "Can you hold."
The head poll worker was a woman named Meeta Sengupta. She was fifty-three years old. She had been a poll worker for nine elections. She had maintained protocol through administrative pressure, weather disruptions, and personal hardship. She looked at Bikram and said: "Yes. We can hold."
At 7:19 PM, the booth officially opened.
At 7:21 PM, the first operative arrived.
He was wearing civilian clothes and had the bearing of someone who worked in institutional administration. He said his name was Rajesh Kumar and that he was from the election commission's supervisory office, here to perform a compliance check.
The head poll worker said: "The compliance check is scheduled for 8:15 PM. The booth is currently in active polling."
The operative said: "The check is being expedited."
Bikram said: "The polling is in progress. The check will occur at the scheduled time. You can wait outside the booth."
The operative looked at him. The operative recognized Bikram. The operative understood that Bikram understood what this was. The operative left.
At 7:34 PM, two operatives arrived together. They said there was a water leak in the building and the booth might need to be evacuated. The head poll worker looked at the ceiling. There was no leak. The operative pointed to a spot on the floor that might have been water but might also have been standard floor humidity.
Bikram said: "The polling continues. If there is an actual structural emergency, the building's administration will evacuate everyone. Until then, you need to exit."
The operatives left.
At 7:52 PM, the pressure became direct.
The operative came in alone. He was no longer using pretense. He was using direct institutional authority. He said the polling needed to stop immediately because there was a discrepancy in the ballot count at another booth and the administration needed to verify the chain of documentation.
Bikram said: "Then the administration can verify the chain of documentation for this booth after the polling completes. The polling continues."
The operative said: "You are preventing an official investigation."
Bikram said: "I am maintaining poll integrity. The investigation can wait two hours."
The operative said: "You will regret this. You understand that. You will regret the decision to protect a fraudulent electoral process."
Bikram said: "The electoral process is not fraudulent. Your attempt to disrupt it makes the opposite statement. Now you need to exit."
The operative stood in the booth for eleven seconds. The power move was designed to establish dominance through physical presence. The problem was that Bikram understood physical presence at a level that made dominance impossible. The operative was trying to establish threat through proximity. The threat was meaningless to someone who had spent his adult life understanding the exact geography of violence.
The operative left.
At 8:04 PM, the polling ended. The count began. Bikram was present for every number. The sealed envelope remained locked in the secured materials box. The evidence was there, documented, part of the official record.
The count was clean. No interference manifested. No pressure altered the outcome. The ballots from Booth 42 were counted correctly.
At 8:47 PM, the count was complete. The results were recorded. The evidence was sealed as part of the official documentation.
The operatives had realized by then that the drive had never been in the courier vehicle. The drive was in the booth. The drive was now part of the electoral record. The drive could not be suppressed without suppressing the entire booth count, and the booth count was already official.
They had failed.

8:52 PM — The Extraction
The problem with stopping the immediate interference was that the operatives had time to pivot. Time to plan a secondary vector. Bikram understood this the moment he left the booth with the sealed materials box and saw three vehicles arranged in the parking area with the specific geometry of interception.
The official commission vehicle that had been scheduled to pick up the materials never arrived. Instead, the three unmarked vehicles formed a box that left only one exit route — the one that required movement through open space, visible and vulnerable.
The operatives had escalated because they could afford to escalate. No civilians were present. The school was closed. The parking area was isolated. The tactical situation was finally in their favor.
Bikram saw the problem and understood the architecture of it.
The sealed materials box was heavy. The evidence was secure but the physical situation was not. He had walked directly from the booth to the parking area because that was what the schedule indicated, and the operatives had used the schedule against him.
One operative was at the vehicle door. Two operatives were at the perimeter, positioned to cut off escape routes. A fourth operative was approaching from the side, moving with the specific coordination that meant either restraint or something more direct, depending on how Bikram responded.
The approach made the architecture clear: they were going to take the materials box and detain him long enough to do so. The detention didn't need to be permanent. It just needed to be long enough.
Bikram did the only thing that was available. He moved.
The materials box was heavy but the side of his body was lighter. He pivoted the weight, drove off his front leg — the right one, which had the best function despite the injury — and covered the distance between himself and the operative at the vehicle door before the operative could respond to the movement.
Contact came from the side. The second operative had accelerated to close the gap. Bikram felt the impact of the operative's shoulder against his left side, against the ribs that were healed at seventy percent and functional at fifty, against the scar tissue that had been holding everything together for six months.
The impact was not severe. The impact was tactical and controlled and designed to create separation.
The impact was enough to light the left ribs into specific pain.
Bikram did not stop moving. He drove the materials box into the operative at the door, using the weight as a bludgeoning tool rather than trying to carry it past. The operative reacted to the collision. The first operative went down. The materials box hit the ground.
The second operative who had impacted Bikram was following up. Bikram pivoted and moved backward, using the vehicle as a barrier, using the space between the car and the operative's position to create distance. The pain in his ribs was information. The pain meant the healing was compromised. The pain also meant the scar tissue was firing, which meant the muscles were responding, which meant he could still move.
The operative was reaching for him. Bikram pivoted again, using the operative's own momentum against him, using the fact that the operative expected Bikram to be compromised. Bikram wasn't compromised. Bikram was damaged and working with the damage, which was different.
The operative hit the side of the vehicle. Hard. The operative recovered immediately — this was trained personnel — but the moment of recovery was enough.
Bikram moved to the materials box, got his hands around it, and moved directly toward the primary exit route. The third operative was still positioned to cut off the route, but the specific geometry of his position meant he was more than eight meters away. Eight meters of open space. Eight meters of vulnerability, but eight meters also meant a run, and a run meant momentum, and momentum meant physics would do some of the work even if his body was compromised.
He ran.
The operative at the exit adjusted his position to intercept. Bikram saw the approach and knew the operative was banking on physical superiority in an open space. The operative was tactical but did not understand what Bikram understood about running with a compromised left side. The operative was expecting pain and fear. The operative was expecting Bikram to be cautious.
Bikram drove directly toward the operative without caution. The left side pain was acknowledged and set aside. The shoulder pain was acknowledged and set aside. The knee was fine — the knee had been rated at eighty percent since the assessment. The knee would do what it needed to do.
Bikram lowered his center and drove through the operative. Not around. Through. The impact was clean, the operative's body deflected by the momentum and the low position, and Bikram came out the other side of the contact still moving toward the exit.
The fourth operative was too far back. The operative had been positioned to cut off a different route. By the time the operative moved to the new vector, Bikram was already at the edge of the parking area, already in motion toward the street where Parveen had positioned the second vehicle exactly as discussed.
He got in. The vehicle moved.
By 8:54 PM, they were three kilometers away and moving toward a safe location on the margins of North Kolkata where Parveen had arranged shelter. The materials box was intact. The evidence was secure. The count from Booth 42 was already in the official record.
The operatives had failed and were now faced with the problem of explaining why they had failed to suppress evidence that was already part of the electoral system.

9:17 PM — Safe Location, North Kolkata
The ribs hurt. The ribs hurt in a specific way that indicated the healing had been compromised. The pain was located in the left side, lower rib section, the exact location of the original fractures. The impact from the operative's shoulder had not re-broken the bone — Bikram would have known if the bone had broken, the sudden sharpness would have been different — but the impact had done enough to the scar tissue and the healing that the next assessment would show degradation.
Bikram understood this. Bikram understood that he had accepted this risk when he made the decision to take the assignment. He understood that the election was protected, that the interference had been stopped, that the evidence was secure.
He also understood that his body was going to file comprehensive dissenting opinions about the decision in the weeks that followed.
Parveen was on her phone to the election commission. The materials box was open. The sealed envelope was being verified. The documentation was being added to the official record.
The commission would acknowledge that interference had been attempted. The commission would document that Bikram had maintained protocol and protected the count. The commission would also, because this is what institutional systems do, begin to ask questions about his judgment, about his willingness to take direct action against operatives, about whether his response had been proportionate or whether it indicated a pattern of escalation.
They had prevented the interference, and the prevention would be documented as potentially excessive force.
Bikram understood this. He understood the architecture of institutional response. He understood that being correct about the threat did not mean the system would trust his judgment about threats.

PART THREE: THE RECKONING
Three Days Later — AIFSFO Office, Kolkata
Colonel Ghosh had the incident report in front of him. The report detailed the operatives' attempts to disrupt the poll. The report detailed Bikram's response. The report also detailed the physical contact in the parking area — the impact with the operative, the impact with Bikram's body, the use of the materials box as a bludgeoning tool, the charge through the operative at the exit.
Colonel Ghosh said: "You were supposed to observe and report. Not engage directly."
Bikram said: "The operatives were engaging directly. The observation role ended when the direct engagement began."
Colonel Ghosh said: "You were cleared for light duty. Light duty does not include physical confrontation with coordinated operatives."
Bikram said: "The polling was under direct threat. The protocol was to maintain the count. Maintaining the count required response."
Colonel Ghosh said: "You also suffered what appears to be a rib injury during the parking lot incident. You are aware that your ribs were structurally compromised."
Bikram said: "Yes."
Colonel Ghosh said: "And you accepted the risk."
Bikram said: "Yes."
Colonel Ghosh sat back. Colonel Ghosh had been watching Bikram closely since Book Two. Colonel Ghosh had authorized the assignment despite the medical restrictions because Colonel Ghosh understood that there were gaps in the system that needed to be filled, and Bikram was one of the people capable of filling them. Colonel Ghosh also understood that authorization came with consequences.
Colonel Ghosh said: "There's going to be a review. The election commission is going to initiate an investigation into the conduct of the booth and the operatives. They're also going to review whether you exceeded your authority in the parking lot."
Bikram said: "I understand."
Colonel Ghosh said: "The incident report says you charged through an operative to reach the exit. The report describes it as aggressive response to attempted material seizure."
Bikram said: "That's accurate."
Colonel Ghosh said: "It's also going to be characterized as pattern-matching bias in your threat assessment. They're going to say you escalated proportionally to what you perceived as threat, not what was actually present."
Bikram said: "The threat was present. The operatives were coordinating for material seizure."
Colonel Ghosh said: "I believe you. The commission is going to struggle with that belief."
He said: "I'm putting an asterisk in your record. Incident report filed. Assessment of operational judgment under threat conditions. Not a formal review. Just a notation that this happened and that you made tactical decisions that, while protective of the democratic process, involved physical engagement beyond standard protocol."
Bikram understood what this meant. An asterisk in an official record was institutional documentation of doubt. The asterisk would not prevent him from future assignments. The asterisk would just mean that future assignments would be scrutinized with additional attention. The asterisk was the system protecting itself by documenting the person who had insisted on protecting it as potentially unreliable.
He said: "I understand."
Colonel Ghosh said: "Do you. Do you understand that being right about a threat does not protect you from the institution's doubt about your judgment regarding threats. That the system will document your correctness in a way that makes your reliability questionable."
Bikram said: "Yes. I understand that now."

Two Months Later — The Investigation
The election commission's investigation documented everything. The operatives' attempts to disrupt the poll. The physical contact in the parking area. The preservation of the electoral record. The evidence of systematic interference.
The investigation also produced the asterisk: Incident report filed. Pattern-matching bias noted. Operational judgment questioned.
The statement was: "While Mr. Dey's actions resulted in the protection of electoral integrity, his willingness to engage directly with operatives suggests a potential pattern of escalation in threat assessment. His background in Para Special Forces may be influencing his perception of administrative pressure as tactical threat. This does not negate the correctness of his actions. It simply suggests that future assessments of his judgment should be conducted with appropriate scrutiny."
Bikram had been right about the election interference.
The system acknowledged this.
And the system also documented that his being right might mean his judgment was questionable.

PART FOUR: THE PHYSICAL COST
The ribs deteriorated over the following weeks. The scar tissue that had been holding them at functional capacity had been compromised by the impact with the operative. The new assessment, conducted two weeks after the election, showed the healing at fifty-five percent instead of seventy. The functional capacity had dropped below forty.
Dr. Wangmo, in the follow-up assessment, said: "You re-fractured them."
Bikram said: "I didn't feel a fracture."
Dr. Wangmo said: "Because the original fractures were scar-tissue fractures, not bone fractures. The scar tissue tore. The underlying healing sustained secondary damage. It's not a re-fracture of the bone. It's a separation of the healing matrix that was holding the fracture together."
She said: "You've set yourself back six weeks. Minimum. If you continue activities that stress that area, you'll set yourself back further."
Bikram said: "How much further."
She said: "Months. You could be looking at two to three additional months of degradation if you continue to subject the area to impact and pressure."
He looked at the imaging. The clean lines of the original fractures had been disrupted. The healing that had occurred around them had been partially separated. The scar tissue was visible as a lighter area on the X-ray, and that lighter area now had secondary fractures running through it.
Dr. Wangmo said: "Whatever you did in that parking lot, it needs to not happen again."
Bikram said: "I understand."
He understood. He also understood that the next assignment that required his specific capabilities would be exactly the kind of assignment that would stress these ribs, and he was not capable of declining such assignments, and the system was not capable of not needing him for such assignments.
The configuration was locked. The consequences were already in motion.

EPILOGUE: TWO MONTHS LATER
Parveen called him in late November, three months after the election night.
She said: "I've found something else. Something that connects to the election interference."
He said: "What do you mean."
She said: "The operatives. The coordination. I traced the funding. I found connections to institutional players. To people in significant positions."
She said: "The election interference was a test. It was practice for something larger."
He said: "What is the larger thing."
She said: "I don't know yet. But I think you should help me find out. I think you should help me investigate."
He was quiet for a long moment. His left ribs were still tender. The follow-up assessment was scheduled in another week. The AIFSFO board was reviewing his incident file and the election commission's report, and the meeting was generating documentation that would follow him through every subsequent official context.
She said: "Are you ready to do that."
